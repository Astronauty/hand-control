\subsection{Comparison against analytic grasp synthesis}
\label{sec:frogger}

We compare against FRoGGeR~\cite{li2023frogger}, which maximizes the min-weight
metric $\bar{\ell}^*$ as its sole objective under a hard floor
$\bar{\ell}^* \ge k_\ell$. Both planners run in one simulator and share the scene,
seeding pool, collision model, execution stack, and scoring instruments; only the
planner differs. Five YCB objects, 20 seeds per object per method, two fingertip
contacts, 200 executed trials. Execution follows FRoGGeR's shaky-pickup protocol:
lift \SI{10}{\centi\meter} in \SI{1}{\second}, hold \SI{1.5}{\second}, with
\SI{3}{\milli\meter} sinusoidal perturbations in all axes from
\SI{0.25}{\second} onward; a pick fails on $>30^\circ$ rotation or
$>\SI{7.5}{\centi\meter}$ deviation. The baseline runs under its own
resample-until-feasible protocol (20 attempts, \SI{60}{\second} budget) from its
bounding-box sampler, while ours takes a single solve from a fixed home
configuration---an asymmetry that favors the baseline.

Three deviations from~\cite{li2023frogger} limit comparability. We simulate at
$\mu = 2.0$ rather than $0.7$, so $\bar{\ell}^*$ values are comparable between
methods on this scene but not to theirs in absolute terms; we use two contacts
against their four Allegro fingers, and their floor $k_\ell = 0.3$ was calibrated at
four; and a two-contact wrench matrix is rank-5-of-6, so the Ferrari--Canny
$\epsilon$ metric is undefined---the grasp wrench set contains no origin-centered
ball in $\mathbb{R}^6$---and we omit that column rather than report a degenerate
value.

\subsubsection{Execution}

Table~\ref{tab:execution-1col} reports per-object outcomes. Aggregated, our method
reaches the lift on $100\%$ $[96,100]$ of trials against $75\%$ $[66,82]$, succeeds
on $85\%$ $[77,91]$ against $35\%$ $[26,45]$, and holds---every fingertip still
carrying load at the end of the lift---on $82\%$ $[73,88]$ against $29\%$
$[21,39]$ ($95\%$ Wilson intervals; all three separate without overlap).

The deficit has two distinct sources. Of 100 baseline trials, 25 never reach the
lift, their solves placing contacts a median of \SI{17.5}{\milli\meter} from the
fingertip surface, and a further 40 grip the object and then lose it. Conditioned on
reaching the lift the baseline succeeds on $47\%$ against our $85\%$, so a single
success rate conflates a reachability failure with a grasp-quality one. The failure
modes also differ in kind: ours are rotation-dominated (12 rotation, 3 deviation),
the expected limitation of a pinch whose wrench matrix cannot resist torque about
the line through its contacts, while the baseline's are deviation-dominated (28,
12)---the object is not held at all. One object resists both methods:
\texttt{036\_wood\_block} requires $\gamma \approx \SI{26}{\newton}$ against
\SIrange{1}{2}{\newton} elsewhere, with baseline solves reaching the
\SI{60}{\newton} stability ceiling, and we read its low rates for both planners as
the two-contact limit rather than a formulation difference.

\subsubsection{Edge-seeking}

Li et al.\ identify edge-seeking---contacts migrating to surface discontinuities,
where the friction cone falls away on both sides---as the dominant failure mode of
both their method and their baseline, and leave combating it to future work without
proposing a metric. We supply one: for each solved contact, the geodesic distance
along the object surface to the nearest point where the surface normal turns by more
than $30^\circ$, searched outward along 16 tangent directions with reprojection at
each step. The test queries the mesh directly, so it is independent of how each
method parameterizes contacts---necessary here, as the baseline's contacts are
forward-kinematics outputs carrying no local surface model.

Table~\ref{tab:edge-1col} reports the result: our contacts sit a median of
\SI{13.5}{\milli\meter} from the nearest edge against \SI{1.0}{\milli\meter}, and
\emph{none} of our 30 contacts fall within \SI{2}{\milli\meter} of an edge against
26 of 30 for the baseline. The \SI{1.0}{\milli\meter} figure is the search step:
those contacts are on the edge, not near it. \texttt{017\_orange} acts as a
control---a near-sphere presents no edge to seek, and it is the one object where the
two methods keep comparable margins. Edge proximity predicts execution outcome:
grasps that held sit a median of \SI{12.0}{\milli\meter} from an edge ($n=18$)
against \SI{1.0}{\milli\meter} for those that failed ($n=12$), a point-biserial
correlation of $r=+0.574$. This is the mechanism behind~\cite{li2023frogger}'s
``yields unstable grasps in practice,'' measured.

\subsubsection{$\bar{\ell}^*$ is a weak predictor of success}

Li et al.\ report $\bar{\ell}^*$ separating successes from failures only weakly.
We reproduce this---pooled medians $0.87$ against $0.52$, $r=+0.317$---and sharpen
it: the correlation is $r=+0.474$ for the baseline but only $r=+0.170$ for ours.
The asymmetry is the point. $\bar{\ell}^*$ is the baseline's own objective, so its
solutions vary along it; ours are near-saturated ($0.88$ for successes against
$0.71$ for failures) and it carries little signal. A metric optimized by one method
is not a neutral instrument for scoring another, and the edge margin above predicts
outcomes better ($r=+0.574$) than $\bar{\ell}^*$ does for either.

\subsubsection{Which mechanisms account for the difference}

Three components bear on the gap. \textbf{Curvature is fitted to local mesh
vertices, not to the SDF Hessian.} An SDF encodes distance to the whole shape, so
its second derivative is global: measured mid-face on a flat side of
\texttt{036\_wood\_block}, where $\kappa=0$, the SDF Hessian reports up to
\SI{9.05}{\per\meter} of spurious curvature sourced from a corner
\SI{50}{\milli\meter} away, while the mesh fit returns exactly zero. Downstream,
fake curvature of $-12.33$ collapsed a trust region to \SI{4.6}{\milli\meter} on a
face with \SI{104}{\milli\meter} of usable surface. A plane-versus-quadratic
selection test accepts curvature only when it earns its residual, which matters on
scanned meshes where a raw quadratic fitted to \SI{1}{\milli\meter} of relief yields
$\kappa \propto 1/r$ and converges at no radius.

\textbf{Each contact carries explicit trust-region bounds}, sized per axis by
marching outward until the paraboloid surrogate departs from the true SDF by a
tolerance; bounds set by measured divergence mark a real boundary and are
distinguished from bounds that merely reach the search cap. This is where the edge
margins of Table~\ref{tab:edge-1col} originate. Two refinements proved necessary: on
a flat face nothing diverges, so the search saturates and reports the cap, which let
contacts walk to a block's top edge where one finger contacted at
\SI{33}{\newton} and displaced the object; and the axis searches bound only the
rectangle's center-lines, leaving corner error at \SI{8.78}{\milli\meter} against a
\SI{4}{\milli\meter} tolerance until a uniform shrink was applied.

\textbf{The commanded internal force is a task-specific certificate re-solved on
achieved geometry}, not a planner output: the linear budget becomes a force box
$m\mathbf{a}$, the angular budget a torque box $I\boldsymbol{\alpha}$, and gravity is
re-datumed about the contact centroid, with infeasibility on any corner returning no
grasp rather than a fabricated force. The allocation is cone-constrained rather than
sign-anchored---an LP enforcing a compressive normal force at every contact inside an
inscribed pyramid, so feasibility implies feasibility for the true cone. On an
asymmetric three-contact grasp the sign-anchored allocation gives
$[1.213, 0.047, 0.325]\,\si{\newton}$ where the cone solve gives
$[1.000, 1.000, 1.000]\,\si{\newton}$.

\subsubsection{Limitations}

The comparison is asymmetric in ways that favor us. Our planner is tuned on this
scene; the baseline is a reimplementation, and one defect in it---contacts reported
at the fingertip site rather than the constrained pad point, displacing every
contact by approximately \SI{10}{\milli\meter}---was found and corrected during this
study, raising its success rate from $7\%$ to $35\%$. We cannot exclude further such
defects, and the $25\%$ of baseline trials placing unreachable contacts is where we
would look first. Friction, contact count, and robustness floor all differ from the
original work, and the plan-time quantities ($\bar{\ell}^*$, edge margin) are
measured over 30 of the 200 trials.
