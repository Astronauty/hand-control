\section{Experiments}
\label{sec:experiments}

We evaluate against FRoGGeR~\cite{li2023frogger}, which optimizes the min-weight
metric $\bar{\ell}^*$ as its sole objective under a hard robustness floor
$\bar{\ell}^* \ge k_\ell$. Both methods are ported into a single simulator, share
one scene, one seeding pool, one collision model, and one execution stack, and are
scored by identical instruments; only the planner differs. Section~\ref{sec:setup}
states the deviations that remain.

\subsection{Setup}
\label{sec:setup}

Five YCB objects on a table, grasped by a Kinova Gen3 with a LEAP hand using two
fingertip contacts ($n=2$), 20 seeds per object per method, for 200 executed
trials. Execution follows FRoGGeR's shaky-pickup protocol: the end-effector is
lifted \SI{10}{\centi\meter} in \SI{1}{\second} and held for \SI{1.5}{\second},
with \SI{3}{\milli\meter} sinusoidal perturbations in all spatial axes applied
from \SI{0.25}{\second} onward. A pick fails if the object rotates by more than
$30^\circ$ or deviates from the pick trajectory by more than \SI{7.5}{\centi\meter}.

FRoGGeR is run under its own synthesis protocol---resample-until-feasible, up to 20
attempts within a \SI{60}{\second} budget---seeded by its oriented-bounding-box
heuristic. Our method is given a single solve from a fixed home configuration with
no sampler. This asymmetry favors the baseline and we report it as such.

Three deviations from~\cite{li2023frogger} affect comparability and we do not
claim otherwise. First, we simulate at $\mu = 2.0$ rather than their $0.7$;
$\bar{\ell}^*$ is strongly friction-dependent, so our values are not comparable to
theirs in absolute terms, only between methods on this scene. Second, we use two
contacts where they use four Allegro fingers, and their floor $k_\ell = 0.3$ was
calibrated at four. Third, a two-contact wrench matrix is rank-5-of-6, so the
Ferrari--Canny $\epsilon$ metric is undefined: the grasp wrench set contains no
origin-centered ball in $\mathbb{R}^6$. We therefore omit the $\epsilon$ column
rather than report a degenerate value, and note that this is a property of the
contact count, not of either method.

\subsection{Execution results}

Table~\ref{tab:execution-1col} reports per-object outcomes. Aggregated over 200
trials, our method reaches the lift phase on $100\%$ $[96, 100]$ of trials against
$75\%$ $[66, 82]$, succeeds on $85\%$ $[77, 91]$ against $35\%$ $[26, 45]$, and
holds---requiring every fingertip to still carry load at the end of the lift---on
$82\%$ $[73, 88]$ against $29\%$ $[21, 39]$. Brackets are $95\%$ Wilson intervals;
all three rates separate without overlap.

\textbf{The deficit has two distinct sources.} Of 100 baseline trials, 25 never
reach the lift at all, and a further 40 grip the object and then lose it.
Conditioned on reaching the lift, the baseline succeeds on $47\%$ against our
$85\%$. The first failure mode is a reachability failure---those solves place
contacts a median of \SI{17.5}{\milli\meter} from the fingertip surface---and only
the second concerns grasp quality. Reporting a single success rate conflates them.

\textbf{The failure modes differ in kind.} Our failures are rotation-dominated
(12 rotation, 3 deviation): the object is held but rotates within the grasp, which
is the expected limitation of a two-contact pinch, whose wrench matrix cannot
resist torque about the line through its contacts. Baseline failures are
deviation-dominated (28 deviation, 12 rotation): the object is not held at all.

\textbf{One object resists both methods.} On \texttt{036\_wood\_block} our success
rate falls to $25\%$ $[11, 47]$ and the baseline's to $10\%$ $[3, 30]$. This object
requires an internal force of $\gamma \approx \SI{26}{\newton}$ against
\SIrange{1}{2}{\newton} for every other object in the set, with baseline solves
reaching the \SI{60}{\newton} stability ceiling, and the object rises a median of
\SI{48}{\milli\meter} of the \SI{100}{\milli\meter} commanded against
\SIrange{95}{97}{\milli\meter} elsewhere. We read this as the two-contact limit on
this scene rather than a difference between formulations.

\subsection{Edge-seeking}

Li et al.\ identify edge-seeking---contacts migrating to surface discontinuities,
where the friction cone falls away on both sides---as the dominant failure mode of
both their method and their baseline, attribute it to the $\epsilon$ metric
preferring large moment arms, and leave combating it to future work. They report no
metric for it. We supply one.

For each solved contact we measure the geodesic distance along the object surface to
the nearest point at which the surface normal turns by more than $30^\circ$,
searching outward along 16 tangent directions and reprojecting to the surface at
each step. The test queries the mesh directly and is therefore independent of how
each method parameterizes its contacts---necessary here, since the baseline's
contacts are forward-kinematics outputs with no local surface model against which a
cheaper test could be defined.

Table~\ref{tab:edge-1col} reports the result. Our contacts sit a median of
\SI{13.5}{\milli\meter} from the nearest edge (IQR \SIrange{12.0}{16.0}{}) against
\SI{1.0}{\milli\meter} (IQR \SIrange{1.0}{1.0}{}) for the baseline, and
\emph{none} of our 30 contacts fall within \SI{2}{\milli\meter} of an edge against
26 of 30 for the baseline. The \SI{1.0}{\milli\meter} figure is the search step
size: those contacts are on the edge, not near it. \texttt{017\_orange} is the
control---a near-sphere presents no edge to seek, and both methods keep comparable
margins there---and the separation appears on the objects that have edges.

Edge proximity predicts execution outcome. Joining plan-time margins to execution
results, grasps that held sit a median of \SI{12.0}{\milli\meter} from an edge
($n=18$) against \SI{1.0}{\milli\meter} for those that failed ($n=12$), a
point-biserial correlation of $r = +0.574$. This is the mechanism
of~\cite{li2023frogger}'s ``yields unstable grasps in practice,'' measured.

\subsection{$\bar{\ell}^*$ is a weak predictor of success}

Li et al.\ report $\bar{\ell}^*$ separating successful from failed picks only
weakly ($0.61$ vs $0.47$ medians, with overlapping interquartile ranges). We
reproduce this and sharpen it. Pooled over both methods, successes have median
$\bar{\ell}^* = 0.87$ and failures $0.52$, with $r = +0.317$. Split by method, the
correlation is $r = +0.474$ for the baseline but only $r = +0.170$ for ours.

The asymmetry is expected and is the point. $\bar{\ell}^*$ is the baseline's own
objective, so its solutions vary along it and it tracks their outcomes; our
solutions are near-saturated in $\bar{\ell}^*$ ($0.88$ for successes against $0.71$
for failures) and it carries little discriminative signal. A metric optimized by
one method is not a neutral instrument for scoring another. We note that the
edge-margin measure above predicts outcomes better ($r = +0.574$) than
$\bar{\ell}^*$ does for either method.

\subsection{Which mechanisms account for the difference}

Three components of our planner bear directly on the gap, and each is independently
motivated.

\textbf{Local surface fits from mesh vertices, not the SDF Hessian.} A signed
distance field encodes distance to the entire shape, so its second derivative is a
global quantity: near any feature it reports curvature belonging to that feature.
Measured mid-face on a flat side of \texttt{036\_wood\_block}, where ground truth is
$\kappa = 0$, the SDF Hessian reports spurious curvature up to
\SI{9.05}{\per\meter}, sourced from a corner \SI{50}{\milli\meter} away, while a
fit to local mesh vertices returns exactly zero. Downstream, fake curvature of
$-12.33$ collapsed a trust region to \SI{4.6}{\milli\meter} on a face with
\SI{104}{\milli\meter} of usable surface. Our fit additionally applies a
plane-versus-quadratic model-selection test, accepting curvature only when the
quadratic improves RMS residual by a threshold, which matters on scanned meshes
where a raw quadratic fitted to \SI{1}{\milli\meter} of scan relief yields $\kappa$
scaling as $1/r$ with fit radius and converging at no radius.

\textbf{Explicit trust-region bounds, which is where the edge margins come from.}
Each contact carries two degrees of freedom on a fitted paraboloid whose extent is
sized per axis by marching outward until the surrogate departs from the true SDF by
a tolerance. Bounds set by measured divergence indicate a real surface boundary;
bounds that reach the search cap indicate only that the surface stayed flat as far
as it was probed, and the two are distinguished rather than conflated. A constant
inset is then held back from every bound. Two refinements were required in
practice: on a flat face nothing ever diverges, so the search saturates and reports
the cap---which permitted contacts to walk to a block's top edge, where a single
finger contacted at \SI{33}{\newton} and displaced the object---and the four axis
searches bound only the rectangle's center-lines, leaving corner error at
\SI{8.78}{\milli\meter} against a \SI{4}{\milli\meter} tolerance until a uniform
shrink was applied.

\textbf{A task-specific wrench certificate, solved on achieved geometry.} The
internal force commanded at execution is not taken from the planner. It is
re-solved from the contact geometry the hand actually reaches, against an explicit
disturbance box: the linear budget becomes a force box $m\mathbf{a}$, the angular
budget a torque box $I\boldsymbol{\alpha}$, and gravity is re-datumed about the
contact centroid. The resulting $\gamma$ is the per-contact internal normal force in
newtons by construction, and infeasibility on any corner of the box returns no
grasp rather than a fabricated force. The allocation itself is cone-constrained
rather than sign-anchored: an LP enforces a compressive normal force at every
contact inside an inscribed pyramidal cone, so feasibility implies feasibility for
the true circular cone. On an asymmetric three-contact grasp the sign-anchored
allocation produced normal forces of $[1.213, 0.047, 0.325]\,\si{\newton}$ where
the cone solve produces $[1.000, 1.000, 1.000]\,\si{\newton}$.

\subsection{Limitations}

The comparison is asymmetric in ways that favor us and we state them plainly. Our
planner is tuned on this scene; the baseline is a reimplementation, and one defect
in it---contacts reported at the fingertip site rather than at the constrained pad
point, displacing every reported contact by approximately
\SI{10}{\milli\meter}---was found and corrected during this study, which raised its
success rate from $7\%$ to $35\%$. We cannot rule out further such defects, and the
$25\%$ of baseline trials that place unreachable contacts is where we would look
first. The friction coefficient, contact count, and robustness floor all differ from
the original work. Finally, $\bar{\ell}^*$ and the edge margin are plan-time
quantities measured over 30 of the 200 trials; extending the edge measurement to the
full seed set would tighten the correlation reported above.
