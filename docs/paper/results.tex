\subsection{Comparison against analytic grasp synthesis}
\label{sec:frogger}

\subsubsection{Relation to our formulation}
\label{sec:frogger-relation}

Both planners score a grasp by the min-weight metric \eqref{eq:minweight}, so the
comparison isolates how that metric is \emph{used} rather than which quantity is
optimized. FRoGGeR solves \eqref{eq:minweight} as an inner problem to optimality at
every iterate and maximizes it as the sole objective under a hard floor
$m\beta^\star \ge k_\ell$, with the configuration as the only decision variable and
contacts pinned to the surface by an equality on the forward kinematics. We instead
embed it (Sec.~\ref{sec:closure}): $\bm{\alpha}$ and $\beta$ are program variables
beside $\bm{q}$ and the patch coordinates $\bm{t}_i$, its equalities are constraints
of \eqref{eq:nlp}, and $J_{\mathrm{gws}}$ is one weighted term among the placement,
regularization and wrench-cone costs.

The embedding trades metric convergence for robustness to initialization, and we
report both directions. Because $\beta$ is one term rather than the whole objective,
it attains the min-weight value only at convergence of the full program: re-solving
\eqref{eq:minweight} exactly on the returned $\bm{W}$ leaves a median optimality gap
of $7.4\times10^{-3}$, nonzero on every solve measured, where an inner solve closes it
by construction. Conversely, opposition between contacts is expressed in our objective
through $J_{\mathrm{align}}$ and $J_{\mathrm{gws}}$ and need not be supplied by the
seed. A \eqref{eq:minweight}-only objective contains no such preference, which is why
FRoGGeR's sampler pre-opens the fingers to the object's bounding-box width and why the
authors report performance to be ``highly sensitive to the sampled initial
conditions''; our planner runs from a fixed home configuration with no sampler.

\subsubsection{Protocol}

Both planners run in one simulator and share the scene, seeding pool, collision
model, execution stack, and scoring instruments; only the planner differs. Four YCB
objects spanning the shape classes that stress a two-contact grasp---a box, a
near-sphere, a thin-walled concave mug, and a flat box \SI{34}{\milli\meter}
deep---with 20 seeds per object per method, 160 executed trials, scored by FRoGGeR's
shaky-pickup protocol: lift \SI{10}{\centi\meter} in \SI{1}{\second}, hold
\SI{1.5}{\second}, with \SI{3}{\milli\meter} sinusoidal perturbation in all spatial
axes, failing on $>30^\circ$ rotation or $>\SI{7.5}{\centi\meter}$ deviation from the
commanded trajectory. The baseline runs its own resample-until-feasible loop (20
attempts within \SI{60}{\second}) seeded by its bounding-box sampler; ours takes a
single solve. We simulate at $\mu = 2.0$, so $\bar{\ell}^*$ is comparable between
methods on this scene but not to absolute values in~\cite{li2023frogger}.

\subsubsection{Execution}

Table~\ref{tab:execution} reports per-object outcomes. Aggregated, our method
reaches the lift on $89\%$ $[80,94]$ of trials against $55\%$ $[44,65]$, succeeds on
$66\%$ $[55,76]$ against $21\%$ $[14,31]$, and holds on $58\%$ $[47,68]$ against
$16\%$ $[10,26]$; all three separate without overlap.

The deficit has two distinct sources: 36 of 80 baseline trials never reach the lift,
their solves placing contacts far enough from the fingertip surface to fail a
pre-squeeze gap check, and a further 27 grip the object and then lose it.
Conditioned on reaching the lift the baseline succeeds on $39\%$ against our $75\%$,
so a single rate conflates a reachability failure with a grasp-quality one.

Per object the two methods separate unevenly. On \texttt{017\_orange} both succeed
and the gap is modest; on \texttt{025\_mug} and \texttt{009\_gelatin\_box} the
baseline essentially fails ($1/20$ and $0/20$) where ours reaches $10/20$ and
$18/20$. \texttt{036\_wood\_block} is hard for both ($5/20$ against $2/20$): it
demands $\gamma \approx \SI{26}{\newton}$ of internal force against
\SIrange{1}{2}{\newton} elsewhere and drives baseline solves to the
\SI{60}{\newton} stability ceiling, which we read as the two-contact limit rather
than a formulation difference. The \emph{held} column is consistently below
\emph{pick} on both methods---most visibly on the mug, $6/20$ against $10/20$---
those being grasps that satisfy the displacement criteria while a fingertip has
unloaded, a property of the criteria rather than of either planner.

\subsubsection{Edge-seeking}

Li et al.\ identify edge-seeking---contacts migrating to surface discontinuities,
where the friction cone falls away on both sides---as the dominant failure mode of
both their method and their baseline, and leave it to future work without proposing a
metric. We supply one: for each contact, the geodesic distance along the surface to
the nearest point where the normal turns more than $30^\circ$, searched along 16
tangent directions with reprojection at each step. It queries the mesh directly and so
is independent of contact parameterization---necessary here, as the baseline's
contacts are forward-kinematics outputs carrying no local surface model.

Table~\ref{tab:edge} reports the result: our contacts sit a median of
\SI{11.0}{\milli\meter} from the nearest edge against \SI{1.0}{\milli\meter}, and
$9\%$ $[4,17]$ of ours fall within \SI{2}{\milli\meter} of an edge against $86\%$
$[77,92]$. \SI{1.0}{\milli\meter} is the search step, so the baseline's contacts are
on the edge, not near it, and its interquartile range is degenerate---the behavior is
not a tail but the norm. Our own margin is object-dependent and smallest on the two
objects with least usable face, \texttt{009\_gelatin\_box}
(\SI{3.5}{\milli\meter}) and \texttt{025\_mug} (\SI{7.0}{\milli\meter}), which
is where our $9\%$ originates. Edge proximity predicts outcome: grasps that held sit
a median of \SI{7.0}{\milli\meter} from an edge ($n=33$) against
\SI{1.0}{\milli\meter} for those that failed ($n=47$), a point-biserial correlation
of $r = +0.389$ over 80 trials. This is the mechanism
behind~\cite{li2023frogger}'s ``yields unstable grasps in practice,'' measured.

\subsubsection{$\bar{\ell}^*$ is a weak predictor of success}

Li et al.\ report $\bar{\ell}^*$ separating successes from failures only weakly.
Pooled over both methods we find medians of $0.91$ against $0.44$
($r = +0.593$), but the aggregate conceals a sharp asymmetry: the correlation is
$r = +0.816$ for the baseline and $r = +0.365$ for ours. Successful grasps score
$0.91$ under both methods; failures score $0.37$ under the baseline but $0.73$ under
ours. $\bar{\ell}^*$ is the baseline's own objective, so its solutions spread along
it and it tracks their outcomes closely; ours are near-saturated and it discriminates
far less. A metric optimized by one method is therefore not a neutral instrument for
scoring another---our failures are not low-$\bar{\ell}^*$ grasps, and no threshold on
$\bar{\ell}^*$ would have predicted them.

\subsubsection{Which mechanisms account for the difference}

Three components of Sec.~\ref{sec:method} bear on the gap. Fitting curvature to local
mesh vertices rather than to the Hessian of the signed-distance field
(Sec.~\ref{sec:contact-param}) avoids the spurious curvature a global field reports
near an unrelated feature, which otherwise collapses the trust region on a flat face.
The per-axis trust region \eqref{eq:trust}, which bounds each patch by where the
surrogate departs from the true surface and distinguishes a measured boundary from an
unprobed one, is what produces the edge margins of Table~\ref{tab:edge}. And the
run-time squeeze is a task-specific certificate re-solved on achieved geometry rather
than a program output, with the cone-constrained allocation \eqref{eq:cone-lp} keeping
every contact loaded inside an inscribed friction cone.

\subsubsection{Limitations}

The comparison is asymmetric in ways that favor us. Our planner is tuned on this
scene; the baseline is a reimplementation, and one defect---contacts reported at the
fingertip site rather than the constrained pad point, displacing each by
approximately \SI{10}{\milli\meter}---was found and corrected during this study,
raising its success rate from $7\%$ to $35\%$. We cannot exclude further such
defects, and the $45\%$ of baseline trials placing unreachable contacts is where we
would look first. Friction and robustness floor differ from the original work, and
plan-time quantities are measured over 80 of the 160 trials. The object set is small
and deliberately weighted toward hard cases, so the absolute rates are lower than a
broader set would give and should be read as a paired comparison rather than as
characteristic success rates.
